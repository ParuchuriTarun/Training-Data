A reviewer marked several comma splices in my paper. What counts as a comma splice, and what are the reliable ways to fix one?
@@@ ASSISTANT
A comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma. An independent clause has a subject and a finite verb and can stand alone as a sentence. In "The deploy failed, the rollback took twenty minutes," both halves can stand alone, so the comma is not enough.

A fused sentence, sometimes called a run-on, has the same problem with no punctuation at all: "The deploy failed the rollback took twenty minutes." The name run-on does not mean the sentence is long; it means the clauses are not properly joined.

There are four standard repairs. Use a period: The deploy failed. The rollback took twenty minutes. Use a semicolon when the ideas are closely linked: The deploy failed; the rollback took twenty minutes. Use a comma plus a coordinating conjunction: The deploy failed, and the rollback took twenty minutes. Or subordinate one clause: Because the deploy failed, the rollback took twenty minutes.

Be careful with words such as however, therefore, and nevertheless. They are conjunctive adverbs, not coordinating conjunctions. "The deploy failed, however the rollback worked" is still a comma splice. Write "The deploy failed; however, the rollback worked" or "The deploy failed. However, the rollback worked."

Also check the comma-plus-conjunction pattern. The conjunction must be one of and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet, and it must join two independent clauses. "The deploy failed, and we rolled back" is correct if we rolled back can stand alone. "The deploy failed and rolled back" is a single clause with a compound predicate, so it needs no comma.

I want to spot logical fallacies in arguments, including my own. Which ones matter most, and how do I repair them?
@@@ ASSISTANT
A logical fallacy is a pattern of reasoning in which the premises do not adequately support the conclusion. Spotting the label is less important than explaining the gap and repairing it. Four patterns cover many everyday cases.

Ad hominem attacks the person instead of the argument: "Her proposal cannot work because she was fired from her last job." The repair is to examine the proposal's costs, evidence, and risks.

Straw man replaces an opponent's claim with a weaker version: "They want to delay the launch, so they must not care about customers." The repair is to state the strongest version of the opposing view and answer that.

False dilemma presents two options when more exist: "Either we cut the budget or we cancel the project." The repair is to identify the missing options, such as reducing scope or phasing the work.

Hasty generalization draws a broad conclusion from too little evidence: "Two users hated the redesign, so the redesign is a failure." The repair is to describe the sample, the selection method, and the limits of the inference.

Other common patterns include slippery slope (one step will inevitably cause an extreme outcome), appeal to popularity (everyone believes it, so it is true), post hoc reasoning (it happened after X, so X caused it), and circular reasoning (the conclusion restates the premise).

Weak: Everyone is switching to this framework, so it must be the right choice.
Strong: The framework is worth testing because three teams cut build times by about 40%, but those teams had small codebases; we should pilot it on one service before committing.

@@@ ASSISTANT

Our checkout has buttons labeled OK, Submit, and Continue. A usability reviewer called them vague. How should I write button labels?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: a button label names the result of pressing it, not the mechanics of pressing it. Start with a verb, keep it to one to three words, use sentence case, and use the same verb for the same action everywhere. "OK", "Submit", "Yes", and "Continue" describe the click; the user has to reread the surrounding text to know what will happen.

Weak: a dialog asks "Are you sure you want to remove this member from the team?" with buttons "OK" and "Cancel".

- Use sentence case and no terminal period: "Save changes", not "Save Changes."

- If the action navigates, write link text that names the destination: "View invoice history", not "Click here".

My instructor says my literature review is "patchwriting". I change the words, so why is it still plagiarism?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: paraphrasing means restating an idea in your own sentence structure and voice while preserving the meaning, and citing the source of the idea. Swapping synonyms while keeping the original's sentence order, clause structure, and distinctive phrasing is patchwriting. It is still too close, and it usually reads badly because the synonyms do not fit the new sentence.

Weak: Original: "Because remote teams lack informal cues, coordination problems often surface only after deadlines slip." Patch: "Since distributed teams are missing casual signals, coordination issues frequently appear only once timelines have slipped." The words changed; the skeleton did not.
Strong: "Deadline slippage is often the first sign of a coordination problem in distributed teams, which lack the informal signals that colocated teams rely on (Chen, 2023)." The order, grammar, and emphasis are new, and the idea is cited.

A method that works:
1. Read the passage until you understand it.
2. Close the source and write the idea from memory in a different order.
3. Compare with the original. If a distinctive phrase survives, either quote it with quotation marks or replace it.
4. Add the citation. Citing an idea does not excuse copying its sentences.

Quote any exact phrase of three or more words, and quote distinctive terms even when they are shorter. Keep a source page number for quotations. For a literature review, most sentences should be your synthesis, not a sequence of one-source summaries.

Answer: "A short feedback turnaround appears to encourage revision: students given comments within two days revised more often than those who waited seven (Ibarra, 2022)." Strong: "Hi Sam, I am sorry your invoice export failed. That is frustrating when you are closing the month. I have cleared the stuck job, so the export should work now. Try it once more, and reply with the time if it fails again. I am watching this ticket until 5 p.m. ET."

The strong version uses the customer's name and symptom, states the fix, gives a next step, and sets a boundary. It does not over-apologize, and it does not claim the problem is permanently solved if it is not.

Build macros with editable slots: [name], [specific issue], [what you did], [next step], [your hours]. Train agents to edit at least the first and last sentence, because those carry most of the human signal. Keep a short list of approved macros rather than hundreds; agents cannot find the right one under pressure.

Answer: "Your refund is processing now. It usually reaches your card in three to five business days, and I will email you when it is done. If you do not see it by Friday, reply here and I will check with the payment team."

I am a freelancer trying to get clients. My cold emails get no replies, or people say they feel spammy. What makes outreach work?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: cold email earns attention with relevance, not volume. The first line proves you looked at their situation specifically. Then one sentence on the problem you solve for people like them, one piece of proof, and a small, clear ask: a 15-minute call, or permission to send one example. Keep it under 120 words, use plain text, and skip images and tracking pixels where you can. The subject line should be specific and honest. Avoid fake familiarity, urgency tricks, and long life stories.

Weak: "Dear Sir or Madam, I hope this email finds you well. I am a highly experienced freelance designer with a passion for excellence. We offer world-class design services at competitive rates. Please find attached my portfolio. Looking forward to hearing from you."
Strong: "Hi Priya, your checkout page keeps the Continue button enabled when the cart is empty; I hit it twice on mobile. I fix conversion leaks like that for Shopify stores. Last month a similar fix raised checkout completion 9 percent for a 40-store client. Want me to send a two-minute video showing the fix? If not, no follow-up."

The observation must be true and specific. Check the site before you send. A generic compliment ("I love your brand") reads as a template; a precise observation proves you did the work.

Follow-up once, after four or five business days, with one new piece of information. Then stop. Honor any "no" immediately and keep a suppression list. Do not use a fake "Re:" subject, a fake mutual connection, or a calendar link in the first email; those raise spam suspicion and waste the reader's time.

Answer: "Could I send you a one-page audit of your checkout flow? If it is useful, we can talk for 15 minutes next week." It offers value first and makes the next step small.

I want to speak at a conference for the first time. How do I write an abstract that gets accepted rather than one that just describes my topic?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: a conference abstract sells a session, not a subject. A strong one names the audience, the concrete problem, what attendees will leave able to do, and why you are credible. Reviewers scan for specificity and fit with the track. Avoid "journey," "lessons learned," and "best practices" without saying which practices. Include format and duration if requested. Aim for 100 to 200 words. The title should promise a specific outcome, not a pun.

Weak: "In today's fast-paced world, testing is more important than ever. In this talk, I will share my journey and lessons learned about quality. Attendees will gain valuable insights."
Strong: "Most teams write end-to-end tests that fail for reasons unrelated to the change: flaky selectors, shared accounts, timing. This talk shows how to cut that noise without losing coverage. You will leave with three patterns: isolating test data per worker, replacing sleeps with network-idle waits, and quarantining flaky tests with an owner and an expiry date. I will show before-and-after runs from a 900-test suite whose flake rate fell from 18 percent to 2 percent over six weeks. For engineers who own CI. 25 minutes, including 5 for questions."

The strong version answers the reviewer's questions: who is this for, what will change for them, and can this person deliver? The numbers make the promise credible, and the duration tells the organizer it fits.

Common fixes:
- Replace the topic with a problem. "Accessibility" becomes "Five accessibility bugs you can fix this sprint."
- Name the takeaway as a capability, not an insight: "You will be able to test focus order," not "You will understand accessibility."
- Drop the autobiography. One line of credibility is enough; the abstract is about the audience.
- Match the track description. If the track asks for hands-on workshops, do not submit a lecture.

Exercise: turn "I want to talk about accessibility" into a title and one learning outcome.
Answer: "Title: Five Accessibility Bugs You Can Fix This Sprint. Outcome: attendees can test keyboard focus order and fix the three most common focus traps." It is specific, scoped, and gives the organizer a reason to schedule it.

I have to give a 12-minute talk and I keep reading my slides. How should I write speaker notes?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: speaker notes are cues for a live performance, not a transcript. Write short lines you can glance at: the opening sentence, the one number that matters, the transition, and the closing ask. Put a full sentence only where the wording is delicate, such as a legal claim or a definition. Mark timing at slide boundaries. Use bold for the anchor word, not whole sentences. Notes should be readable at a glance while standing, so eight to twelve words per cue is plenty. Rehearse from the notes, then cut them in half.

Weak: "As I mentioned earlier, our platform has been designed from the ground up to deliver a seamless and scalable experience for customers across a wide range of industries and use cases."
Strong: "Point: one dashboard, three teams. Number: 40 percent less reporting time. Transition: 'That is the what. Here is how we got there.'"

Also note what is on screen, because the slide and the words should not duplicate each other: "Slide 4: p99 chart. Say: 'The tail, not the average.'" Put sources and pronunciation guides in the notes, never on the slide. Add a "cut if late" marker to one section, so you have a planned way to recover.

For a 12-minute talk, write roughly 1,400 to 1,700 spoken words, but do not script them unless you need exact phrasing. Rehearse with a timer twice: once to learn the shape, once to test the cuts. If a sentence is hard to say aloud, it is too long; split it.

Exercise: write a note for a slide showing a 30 percent drop in cost per ticket.
Answer: "Anchor: '30 percent lower cost per ticket.' Proof: 12 months, four teams. Transition: 'The savings came from one change.'" The note gives the speaker the number, the evidence, and the next move without a paragraph to read.

Our post-purchase survey asks "How much did you love our fast, friendly service?" and the results are useless. How do I write a neutral question?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: one idea per question, neutral wording, balanced response options, and no assumed answer. Remove loaded adjectives such as "fast" and "friendly," double-barreled pairs such as service and speed, absolutes such as "always," and leading scales. Give a timeframe and a reference point. Offer "Not sure" or "Do not know" when it is a real answer, and "Prefer not to say" for sensitive items. Test the question with five people and ask what they thought it was asking.

Weak: "How much did you love our fast, friendly service? [A lot / Very much / Extremely]"
Strong: "Thinking about your most recent visit, how satisfied were you with the speed of service? [Very satisfied / Somewhat satisfied / Neither satisfied nor dissatisfied / Somewhat dissatisfied / Very dissatisfied / Do not know]"

The strong version fixes four problems: it sets a timeframe, removes the compliment, asks about one attribute, and offers a full scale including a neutral midpoint and a "don't know." If you also care about friendliness, ask a separate question; combining them makes the answer uninterpretable.

More fixes:
- Replace agreement scales with frequency or satisfaction when agreement is ambiguous. "I am satisfied..." is not the same as "The checkout was fast."
- Avoid negatives that create double negatives: "How often do you disagree that support was not helpful?"
- Keep demographic questions optional and at the end, with a "prefer not to say" option.
- Do not ask "Why?" as the only follow-up; pair it with a specific prompt: "What would have made that easier?"

Exercise: fix "Don't you agree our new checkout is better?"
Answer: "How would you compare the new checkout with the previous one? [Much better / Somewhat better / About the same / Somewhat worse / Much worse / Did not use the previous checkout]." It removes the presupposition and includes people who cannot compare.

I am running my first usability test. How do I write tasks that do not accidentally give away the answer?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: a task states a goal and a context, not a path. Name the user's situation and what they want to achieve; never name the menu, button, or page. Do not ask a leading question such as "Isn't it easy to...?" Let the participant find the route. Keep success criteria for the moderator, not the participant. Start with an easy warm-up, then realistic tasks, and avoid tasks that depend on data the participant does not have. Pilot with one person and remove any word that points to the interface.

Weak: "Click Settings, then Privacy, then toggle off marketing emails. Did you find it easily?"
Strong: "You have been getting promotional emails and want them to stop. Show me what you would do. (Success: marketing toggle off or unsubscribe confirmed. Stop after 3 minutes.)"

The strong version gives a goal and a reason, not a route. It also tells the moderator what counts as success and when to stop, so the session does not drift.

Moderator behavior matters as much as the wording. Read the task exactly as written, then stay quiet. If the participant asks for help, respond neutrally: "What would you do next?" or "What are you thinking?" Do not rescue them before the time limit. After the task, ask "How easy or difficult was that?" rather than "Did you like it?"

Watch for tasks that test memory of the UI rather than the design. "Find where you changed your password last week" tests recall; "Change your password" tests the interface. Also avoid tasks that require the participant to know internal names: "Use the asset manager" only works if that label is visible and familiar.

Exercise: rewrite "Find the Export button in the Reports tab and download a CSV" as a task.
Answer: "You need to send last month's report to your accountant as a spreadsheet. Show me how you would get that file." It states the goal and the format, but not the tab, button, or sequence.

The strong entry settles the argument because it gives a test, not a vote. A writer can apply it without asking. The exception prevents a false correction when quoting someone else's UI.

We publish product docs translated from German by machine translation. The grammar is correct but it sounds wrong. How do I edit it into natural English?
@@@ ASSISTANT
Principle: machine translation preserves source structure, so the target reads as English words in German order. Edit for meaning first, then idiom, then style, not word by word. Watch for verb-final order carried over, stacked compound nouns, missing or wrong articles, literal prepositions, and false friends. Keep product terms consistent with the glossary. Do not add claims the source does not make. Check numbers, names, and units last.

Weak: "The configuration can by the administrator in the settings be changed."
Strong: "An administrator can change the configuration in Settings."

Weak: "Please inform us your feedback until Friday."
Strong: "Please send us your feedback by Friday."

The first pair fixes word order and the preposition. The second fixes a literal translation: German "bis Freitag" becomes "by Friday," not "until Friday," and "inform us your feedback" needs "of" or, more naturally, "send us."

A method that catches more than grammar:
1. Read the paragraph without looking at the source and summarize it aloud.
2. Rewrite from your summary, not from the German sentence.
3. Compare with the source for facts, names, and numbers.
4. Read the result aloud; if you run out of breath or stumble, the sentence is too long.
5. Check glossary terms and UI labels against the product.

Do not "improve" the content while editing. If the German says "may," do not upgrade it to "will." If a sentence is vague in the source, flag it to the author rather than inventing detail.

Exercise: fix "Make a photo from the settings page and send it to us per email."
Answer: "Take a photo of the settings page and email it to us." "Make a photo" is a Germanism; English takes a photo. "Per email" becomes "email" or "by email," and the sentence loses the unnecessary preposition.
